% ---------------- ABSTRACT ----------------
\begin{abstract}
Latency-based side-channel leakage has emerged as a major security concern in modern machine-learning-as-a-service (MLaaS) deployments, where models are exposed through black-box query-response interfaces. Prior work on neural network reverse engineering has largely focused on hardware traces, electromagnetic leakage, cache behavior, or output-based model extraction. In contrast, this work investigates whether inference latency alone can be exploited to fingerprint neural network architectures under constrained black-box access settings.
Statistical timing descriptors including mean latency, standard deviation, tail latency, skewness, kurtosis, and temporal autocorrelation are extracted from repeated inference observations and used to train lightweight architecture classifiers. Experimental results demonstrate that architectures including VGG, ResNet, MobileNetV2, and InceptionV3 produce statistically distinguishable timing signatures, enabling effective identification using latency information alone. Additional experiments analyze query-count sensitivity and robustness under noisy timing conditions. The findings further demonstrate that hardware environment heterogeneity substantially reduces fingerprinting transferability, a result with direct implications for MLaaS defense design.
\end{abstract}

% ---------------- INTRODUCTION ----------------
\section{Introduction}

Most cloud platforms and machine learning services provide hosted models through API-based interfaces. These services permit users to query deployed models through query-response interfaces. These systems generally run in black-box deployment environments where users have limited visibility into the underlying architecture or implementation characteristics. In settings like these, systems may unintentionally leak information through side channels such as inference latency~\cite{kocher,csinn,host2020}. 


Recent work has shown that model behavior, query patterns, hardware traces, and implementation-level signals can expose architectural  details about neural networks\cite{prada,deepsniffer,csinn,host2020}.However, many existing approaches rely on hardware-level traces, electromagnetic leakage, or richer side-channel information beyond simple request-response timing. These risks are especially relevant as machine-learning-as-a-service platforms increasingly expose high-value models through black-box interfaces.This raises an important question: whether inference latency alone can reveal architecture-level information under constrained black-box access conditions.

 In this work we specifically investigate inference latency as a possible side-channel for neural network architecture fingerprinting. We methodically investigate whether the response time of neural network inference can reveal structural information about the underlying model, which may subsequently assist surrogate model construction and model stealing attacks~\cite{knockoff,prada}. We hypothesize that differences in the computational graph topology across architectures like VGG, ResNet, MobileNetV2 and Inception can leak distinct latency signatures that can be exploited for model fingerprinting.


\subsection{Research Gap and Contributions}

Existing research on reverse engineering of neural networks and model extraction have relied upon hardware trace data, electromagnetic emission data, cache data or output based extraction. In comparison to this body of research, there is relatively little existing research examining if only inference latency could provide a means of deriving architecture level information in situations where the user has very restricted black box access. The objective of this work is to investigate if computational graph structure produces timing signatures which are statistically distinguishable from one another and therefore exploitable as a basis for identifying neural network architectures solely through request response timing observation.

The primary contributions to this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item We present Neural Architecture Fingerprinting (NAF) as a latency-only Black-Box Side Channel Problem.
\item We determine if the Computational Graph Topology is sufficient to induce Statistically Distinguishable Timing Signatures in VGG, ResNet, MobileNetV2 and InceptionV3 architectures.
\item We test both Statistical Latency Descriptors (Mean Latency, Variance, Skewness, Kurtosis), and Temporal Latency Descriptors (Autocorrelation).
\item We assess how Query Count and Noisy Timing Conditions impact NAF Performance.
\item We will also address Security Implications, Limitations, and Countermeasures to prevent Timing-Based Architecture Leakage.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs)}
Modern neural network architectures can be modeled as DAGs, where the execution flow propagates through interconnected computational layers. Sequential models such as VGG primarily show a linear computational graph, whereas architectures such as ResNet and Inception exhibit branching, merging, and parallel execution as they operate on the principles of skip connections and multi-scale feature extraction \cite{resnet,inceptionv3}. These core structural differences alter the execution traits of the model, including inference latency, synchronization overhead, and computational variability. Consequently, DAG topology becomes an important factor in understanding latency-based side-channel behavior in neural network systems.

\subsection{Side-Channel Attacks}

Side-channel attacks exploit indirect execution signals such as timing behavior, hardware traces, cache activity, or memory-access patterns to infer information about a target system~\cite{kocher,host2020}. In machine learning systems, observable execution behavior may unintentionally leak architectural or implementation-level information about deployed neural networks~\cite{deepsniffer,csinn}. Inference latency represents one of the most accessible side channels in black-box ML deployments because response timing can often be measured without privileged hardware access.

Different neural network architectures exhibit distinct execution behavior due to variations in computational graph topology, synchronization behavior, branching patterns, and computational complexity. These topology differences may therefore induce statistically distinguishable timing signatures across deployed neural network architectures.


Recent studies on neural-network reverse engineering and model extraction have shown that architectural hints, hardware traces, and query observations can leak information about deployed neural networks \cite{csinn,deepsniffer,dnnreverse,host2020}. As a result, different architectures may produce distinct latency distributions during inference. In black-box deployment settings, an adversary may repeatedly query the model and analyze these timing variations to infer structural information about the underlying architecture.

\subsection{Black-Box Setting}

The experiment presumes a black-box deployment environment in which the attacker has no visibility into the internal architecture, parameters, data used in training, or implementation insights of the deployed neural network model. The target is accessible solely through a request-response interface. Despite complete restriction from internal access, the adversary can repeatedly query the model and observe inference response times. These timing records are further analyzed in order to infer structural characteristics of the underlying model.

query the target system and record inference response times.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Model Selection}
Four convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures representing distinct computational graph topologies were selected for evaluation: ResNet-18, ResNet-50, VGG-16, InceptionV3, and MobileNetV2. They were selected because they exhibit distinct execution patterns and computational graph structures that may influence inference latency behavior. They were loaded from\texttt{torchvision.models}~\cite{vgg,resnet,inceptionv3} and were selected because they represent significantly different computation graph topologies.VGG primarily follows a sequential execution structure, whereas ResNet introduces skip connections and residual blocks that create branch-and-merge computation paths. MobileNetV2 utilizes depthwise separable convolutions optimized for lightweight inference \cite{mobilenetv2}. InceptionV3 employs parallel branches and concatenation, resulting in highly parallel execution paths.

These architectural differences make the selected models suitable for evaluating whether computational graph structure influences inference latency and timing behavior.

\subsection{Latency Measurement}

Inference latency measurements were conducted on an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H CPU with 16GB RAM running Ubuntu 22.04 and PyTorch 2.2. Additional experiments were performed using CUDA-enabled GPU inference in order to evaluate latency fingerprinting behavior across heterogeneous hardware environments. Baseline experiments were conducted on CPU execution with batching disabled, while additional heterogeneous hardware experiments were conducted using CUDA-enabled GPU inference. All models received identical input tensors of size \texttt{(1,3,224,224)} to ensure consistent evaluation conditions. Each model was switched to evaluation mode using \texttt{model.eval()} in order to disable training-specific behavior during inference. In addition, \texttt{torch.no\_grad()} was utilized to disable gradient computation and reduce unnecessary overhead during latency measurement. A warm-up inference pass was executed prior to timing measurements to reduce initialization overhead.

Each architecture executed 100 inference cycles, with individual inference times recorded using \texttt{time.perf\_counter()}. The duration of every forward pass was measured in milliseconds, producing a latency distribution for each architecture. Statistical and temporal timing descriptors including mean latency, standard deviation, minimum latency, maximum latency, P95 latency, skewness, kurtosis, and lag-1 autocorrelation were subsequently extracted from the latency distributions. \subsection{Feature Extraction}

Raw latency measurements were transformed into statistical and temporal timing descriptors for architecture fingerprint generation. For a latency sequence \(L = \{l_1, l_2, ..., l_n\}\), features including mean latency, standard deviation, minimum latency, maximum latency, P95 latency, skewness, kurtosis, and lag-1 autocorrelation were extracted from the recorded inference timings. These descriptors were used to generate timing fingerprints for downstream architecture classification tasks.

\subsection{Classification Approach}

Latency-derived feature vectors were used to train lightweight architecture classification models. Each feature vector represented the statistical timing characteristics of a specific neural network architecture including ResNet-18, ResNet-50, VGG-16, MobileNetV2 and InceptionV3.

Model evaluation was performed using 5-fold cross-validation with an 80/20 train-test split across folds. Prior to classification, feature normalization was applied in order to reduce scale variation across different statistical descriptors. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Logistic Regression classifiers were subsequently trained and evaluated on the extracted latency features. Performance was evaluated using mean classification accuracy, macro-F1 score, confusion matrices, and per-class precision-recall metrics across validation folds. KNN was evaluated using $k=5$, while Logistic Regression was configured with standard L2 regularization.

Classification accuracy, confusion matrices, and classification reports were utilized to evaluate the effectiveness of latency-based architecture fingerprinting. Figure~\ref{fig:classification_pipeline} illustrates the classification workflow implemented during the experiments.

\subsection{Effect of Query Count}

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy_vs_query_count} summarizes classification performance under varying query budgets. Under controlled CPU execution, single-query observations achieved 98.2\% accuracy, likely reflecting strong mean-latency separation between architectures in an uncontended environment. This sensitivity degrades substantially under realistic multi-hardware conditions, suggesting that query-count alone is not the primary bottleneck for practical fingerprinting — execution environment consistency is. Classification performance gradually decreased as larger independent query windows were utilized. This likely reflects that larger query windows average out localized latency spikes that contribute to inter-architecture separability.

The heterogeneous hardware experiments showcase that latency-based neural network fingerprinting remains somewhat partially effective across different execution environments, although hardware-specific timing behavior introduces additional variability that reduces classification separability under mixed deployment conditions. % ---------------- DISCUSSION ----------------
\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
The observed accuracy difference between controlled CPU-only evaluation (95\%) and mixed hardware conditions (43\%) represents a fundamental property of timing-based side channels: latency fingerprints are partially hardware-environment-specific rather than purely architecture-specific. This result highlights that hardware heterogeneity and inference routing variability may function as partial mitigations against timing-based architecture leakage in MLaaS deployment settings.
The experimental results demonstrate that inference latency preserves architecture-dependent timing characteristics that remain exploitable under black-box conditions. Distinct latency distributions were observed across all evaluated architectures, and meaningful classification performance was retained even under injected timing noise. The evaluation was conducted under relatively controlled execution conditions, however, and may not fully capture real-world deployment effects such as cloud scheduling behavior, multi-tenant inference workloads, and network-induced timing variability.
Several countermeasures against timing-based architecture leakage are identified. Latency normalization eliminates timing variance by padding inference responses to a fixed response time. Random latency injection introduces artificial noise to obscure architecture-specific timing signatures. Hardware routing randomization reduces cross-query timing consistency, as suggested by the degraded classification performance observed under mixed hardware conditions. Rate limiting and query anomaly detection can additionally constrain the number of timing observations available to an adversary.

% ---------------- FUTURE WORK ----------------
\section{Future Work}

Future work may extend the proposed methodology to larger-scale heterogeneous deployment environments including cloud inference systems, edge accelerators, and mobile devices in order to evaluate the consistency of latency-based fingerprinting under realistic multi-platform execution conditions. Additional investigation may also include larger neural network families such as transformer architectures, hybrid models, and quantized networks, along with more advanced temporal and frequency-domain timing descriptors. Evaluating the effects of realistic deployment conditions including network jitter, concurrent workloads, caching behavior, and asynchronous execution pipelines may further improve understanding of timing leakage in practical ML deployment environments. Future research may additionally explore defensive mechanisms designed to mitigate timing-based information leakage during neural network inference.
